1. Purpose and product direction

inFORMed is envisaged as a modular legal assistance platform that helps people understand and navigate legal processes, prepare the information required for applications, and generate relevant legal documents.

The initial module will focus on helping people prepare protection order applications. The platform should be designed to accommodate additional legal tools in the future without requiring a complete rebuild for each new service.

Core product principle
inFORMed should combine structured legal workflows, verified legal information and document automation, supported by AI where it adds value. AI should assist the process, but should not become the authority that determines a person's legal rights or makes legal decisions.

The platform should help users complete the relevant steps while keeping them in control of the information they provide and the documents they prepare. It should not invent facts, guarantee legal outcomes or present unverified AI interpretations as established law.

2. Proposed platform architecture

The architecture separates the user experience, legal workflows, legal rules, legal knowledge, document generation and secure case information. This separation makes the system easier to maintain, validate and extend to other legal processes.

Conceptual architecture. Components may be implemented as services or modules within the existing application infrastructure.

2.1 User interface

The interface should provide a clear, accessible and supportive experience. Users may be completing a legal application under stressful circumstances, so questions should be easy to understand, presented in manageable sections and accompanied by clear explanations.

2.2 Workflow engine

The workflow engine manages the application process. It determines which questions are presented, in what order, and which additional questions are required based on a user's answers. It should support conditional pathways rather than forcing every applicant through an identical questionnaire.

2.3 Legal rules engine

This component applies approved legal and procedural rules. It identifies applicable requirements, required information, exceptions and relevant workflow paths. Rules should be traceable to their legal sources and approved by designated legal reviewers.

2.4 Legal knowledge service

This service makes verified legal information available to the platform. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) may be used to retrieve relevant legislation, regulations, official forms and approved explanations. Retrieved information should include source references and should not override explicit workflow rules.

RAG means that, before the AI answers, the system searches the approved legal knowledge for the passages that are relevant to the question and gives only those passages to the AI, together with their references. The AI then answers from those passages rather than from its general training. For a single legal module with a small, approved knowledge base, the whole approved knowledge can instead be given to the AI on every turn, which removes the risk of a search missing the right passage. Retrieval becomes necessary as the knowledge grows, for example when several legal modules exist or when the platform includes a directory of courts, police stations and support services. In both cases only approved content is used, and every answer can be traced to its source.

2.5 Document generation and review

Information collected through the workflow is mapped to the relevant official templates. The system checks whether required fields have been completed, generates draft documents and gives the user an opportunity to review and correct them before export.

2.6 Secure data and audit layer

This layer protects personal case information, manages access and records important actions. It should support appropriate retention and deletion, and distinguish individual case records from the platform's general legal knowledge.

The simplest and safest design keeps case information on the applicant's own device, so that nothing about their case is held by GRIT. This layer becomes essential if a later decision is made to store applications, for example so that a paralegal can review them or so that an applicant can continue on another device. In that case, every record is encrypted, stored in South Africa, deleted after an approved period, and every view, change and export is logged with who did it and when.

3. Data framework

Unlike a conversational support system that may rely heavily on community knowledge, inFORMed requires structured legal and procedural information. Data should be organised according to its purpose and kept in separate repositories or logical data domains.

Data categoryExamples of informationPurpose
Legislation and legal sourcesActs, amendments, regulations, relevant legal sections and official guidance.Establishes the legal foundation and source of authority.
Official documentsApplication forms, affidavits, notices, supporting forms and document instructions.Supports accurate preparation of official documents.
Legal proceduresApplication steps, filing requirements, deadlines, court processes and procedural guidance.Defines the process users need to navigate.
Legal rulesConditions, exceptions, required information, decision pathways and validation rules.Controls workflow logic and determines which requirements apply.
Court and service informationCourt locations, contact details, operating procedures and verified support services.Helps users understand where and how to take the next steps.
Plain-language explanationsApproved explanations of legal terms, rights, processes and document requirements.Makes legal information easier to understand.
Workflow examplesApproved scenarios, alternative pathways and examples of missing or incomplete information.Supports workflow design and testing.
Evaluation dataTest cases, expected outputs, edge cases and failure scenarios.Measures accuracy, completeness, consistency and safety.

The categories above have different owners and different rules. Legislation, official documents, procedures, rules and explanations are not personal information: they can be collected widely, but every item must be accurate, current and approved by a legal reviewer. Evaluation data is created specifically for testing and must never contain real people's cases. Individual case information, described below, is the opposite: it is highly sensitive and should be collected only to the extent the legal process requires.

3.1 Individual application and case information

Information entered by a user during an application must be treated separately from the legal knowledge repository. Depending on the applicable legal process, this may include:

  • Applicant and respondent details.
  • The relationship between the applicant and respondent, where legally relevant.
  • The applicant's description of incidents, including dates and locations where known.
  • Information about children or dependants, where relevant.
  • Details of the protection being requested.
  • Supporting information and evidence details.
  • Information required to complete the applicable official forms.
Data separation: Individual case information is not community knowledge. It should not automatically be added to the RAG repository, shared across cases or used to train AI models. Access and processing must be governed by the approved privacy, security and legal requirements.

4. Legal knowledge repository structure

Each legal source should have sufficient metadata to establish where it came from, which jurisdiction it applies to, whether it is current and whether it has been legally reviewed.

For example, a record for the protection order application form could look like this:

{
  "document_id": "za-po-form6-j480e",
  "legal_tool": "protection_order",
  "jurisdiction": "South Africa",
  "document_type": "official_form",
  "title": "Form 6 and Form 6A: Application for a protection order (J480E)",
  "source_url": "https://www.justice.gov.za",
  "legal_reference": "Domestic Violence Regulations, Form 6 and Form 6A",
  "effective_date": "2025-03-07",
  "version": "J480E, March 2025",
  "reviewed_by": "GRIT legal reviewer",
  "review_date": "to be completed at sign-off",
  "approval_status": "under_review",
  "content": "Field list, section headings and instructions extracted from the official form"
}

Only records with the status "approved" are made available to users or to the AI. Every record should also have a next review date, so that legal content is checked regularly and not only when someone notices a change.

Superseded legal sources should be retained for audit and historical traceability where appropriate, but must not be presented as current guidance. The system should have a controlled process for reviewing and updating legal content when laws, regulations or official forms change.

5. Protection order application workflow

The first legal module should be mapped from the actual legal process and current official forms. The workflow below is an initial product model and must be validated by GRIT's legal team before implementation.

  1. Start applicationExplain the purpose and limitations of the tool, provide the relevant privacy information and obtain appropriate consent.
  2. Identify the applicantEstablish whether the person is applying for themselves or assisting someone else, and identify any additional requirements for that pathway.
  3. Understand the circumstancesCollect relevant facts through carefully designed questions. Allow the applicant to describe events in their own words.
  4. Identify the applicable legal pathwayUse legally approved rules to identify the relevant requirements and any additional questions.
  5. Identify the protection requestedExplain the available options under the applicable law and allow the applicant to indicate what they are seeking.
  6. Collect supporting informationGather the information required for the application and any supporting documents.
  7. Generate the applicationMap validated answers to the appropriate official templates and produce draft documents.
  8. Review and exportAllow the applicant to review and correct the documents, then download them with clear information about the next steps.

5.1 How the workflow relates to the official form

The current official application is Form 6, with the applicant's confidential personal details on Form 6A (court form J480E, March 2025). The workflow steps above gather the information for its ten sections:

Form 6 sectionInformation requiredWorkflow step
1. Applicant detailsNames, identity number or date of birth, contact details, address, relationship to the respondentIdentify the applicant
2. Person applying on behalf of the applicantName, capacity, reason, written consent of the applicant where requiredIdentify the applicant
3. Respondent detailsName, relationship, identity details if known, where the respondent can be foundUnderstand the circumstances
4. Other people affectedChildren and other people affected, and howUnderstand the circumstances
5. Description of the domestic violenceHistory of abuse, specific incidents with dates, places, injuries and treatmentUnderstand the circumstances
6. UrgencyWhy an interim order is needed, weapons, threats, fearIdentify the applicable legal pathway
7. Orders requestedWhat the respondent should be prohibited from doingIdentify the protection requested
8. Additional conditionsSeizure of weapons, police assistance, rent, maintenance, emergency monetary relief, keeping the address confidentialIdentify the protection requested
9. Personal propertyBelongings to be collectedIdentify the protection requested
10. Police station, court and documentsPolice station for reporting a breach, court, documents attachedCollect supporting information

Sections 5, 6 and 7 carry the most weight, because the magistrate decides mainly on what happened, why it is urgent and what the applicant is asking for. They are also where applicants most need support, because people rarely tell their story in the order a form expects.

The system should preserve the applicant's original account. AI may help organise information into relevant sections, but it must not add facts, strengthen allegations or convert uncertainty into certainty. The applicant should be able to review and correct any AI-assisted wording.

Where relevant, the system should distinguish between what the applicant personally experienced, what another person told them, what they believe happened and what they are unsure about.

6. Reusable modular design for future legal tools

The platform should use shared services while keeping each legal process independently configurable. This will allow new legal tools to be added without rebuilding the core platform.

Shared platform and independent legal modules

↓
Protection order moduleIts own legal sources, rules, workflow, templates and evaluation tests
Future legal module AIndependent legal content, process and validation
Future legal module BIndependent legal content, process and validation
Future legal module CIndependent legal content, process and validation

Shared infrastructure supports multiple legal tools, while each tool maintains its own legal rules and knowledge boundaries.

Each legal module should have its own:

  • Legal knowledge repository and approved sources.
  • Jurisdiction and applicable legislation.
  • Workflow definitions and branching logic.
  • Legal rules and validation requirements.
  • Official document templates.
  • Evaluation dataset and test scenarios.
  • Designated legal reviewer and approval process.

Shared services should be reusable, but retrieval and access controls must prevent one legal module from unintentionally using another module's legal content, rules or case records.

7. Role of AI within inFORMed

AI should be used where it improves accessibility, comprehension and the handling of natural-language information. It should not replace explicit legal rules or the authority of legal professionals and courts.

FunctionProposed approach
Guiding users through questionsStructured workflows, with AI-assisted clarification where appropriate.
Understanding free-text descriptionsNatural-language processing and AI, with user confirmation.
Identifying missing informationRules-based validation.
Determining which questions applyApproved legal rules and workflow logic.
Explaining legal terms and processesRAG using verified legal sources and approved plain-language content.
Identifying relevant legal provisionsRetrieval with source references and legal validation.

Making legal decisionsReserved for the appropriate human or legal authority, not the AI system.

8. Privacy, security and responsible data handling

Protection order applications may contain identity information, allegations of abuse, health information, information about children and potentially sensitive evidence. Privacy and security requirements must therefore be incorporated into the architecture from the beginning.

  • Data minimisation: Collect only information required for the relevant legal process or clearly justified product function.
  • Encryption: Protect information in transit and at rest.
  • Access control: Limit access to authorised people based on role and legitimate need.
  • Data separation: Keep legal knowledge, evaluation data and individual case records separate.
  • Consent and transparency: Explain what information is collected, why it is needed and how it will be used.
  • Retention and deletion: Establish and implement approved retention periods and deletion processes.
  • Auditability: Record relevant access, changes, exports and administrative actions.
  • AI processing controls: Assess and approve any transfer of sensitive information to external AI services.
  • Training restrictions: Do not use identifiable case information to train external models without the necessary legal basis and written approval.

Data protection requirements, including applicable requirements under POPIA and other relevant laws, should be reviewed with the appropriate legal and data protection leads before deployment.

8.1 What this means in practice

  • Special personal information: POPIA gives extra protection to certain kinds of information, including information about health and about alleged criminal behaviour. Descriptions of abuse and injuries fall into this category, so the platform needs a clear legal basis for processing them.
  • Children's personal information: Information about children affected by the abuse is also specially protected and must be handled with particular care.
  • External AI services: When an AI model hosted outside South Africa processes what an applicant writes, personal information is transferred across borders. This must be assessed before deployment, with an agreement in place with the provider that sets out how the information is handled and confirms it is not used to train their models.
  • Information Officer: GRIT's Information Officer is responsible for POPIA compliance and should sign off the data protection assessment before any pilot with real applicants.
  • Privacy notice: Applicants should be told, in plain language and in their own language, what is collected, who can see it, how long it is kept, how to delete it, and the risks of using a shared phone.

9. Development and implementation phases

The work is organised in six phases. Each phase has a clear purpose, a set of activities, defined outputs and a point at which it is considered complete. Later phases depend on the approvals from earlier ones, so legal accuracy is built in from the start rather than checked at the end.

  1. Legal process discoveryWork with GRIT's legal experts to map the protection order process, including exceptions, supporting documents and what happens after an application is prepared.

    Purpose

    To understand the full legal journey from the applicant's point of view before deciding what the system should do. The protection order process does not end when the form is completed: the applicant still has to have it commissioned, see the magistrate, arrange service on the respondent and return to court for the final order.

    Activities

    • Workshops with GRIT's legal team to walk through the process step by step, from the first decision to apply to the final order and what happens if the order is breached.
    • Mapping the different pathways: applying for oneself, a helper applying on someone's behalf, applications involving children, urgent and after-hours applications, and situations where there is no domestic relationship and another process applies.
    • Identifying the decision points where the applicant needs information, the points where the clerk or magistrate decides, and the points where a referral is needed.
    • Listing every document involved at each step: the application, the applicant's confidential details, supporting affidavits, the interim order, the notice of the return date, the final order and the warrant of arrest.
    • Reviewing the existing prototype and GRIT's current guides against the mapped process to see what is already covered and what is missing.
    • Agreeing the scope of the first version: which process, which jurisdiction, and whether the tool prepares documents only.

    Outputs

    • A process map of the protection order journey, including exceptions and what happens after submission.
    • A list of decision points, branches and referral points.
    • An agreed scope statement for the first version.
    • Answers, or owners and dates, for the questions in section 10.

    Who is involved

    GRIT's legal team, the AI Lead, the safeguarding lead, and where possible a paralegal or court support worker who sees applicants in practice.

    Complete when: the legal team has reviewed and agreed the process map and the scope of the first version.

  2. Source and data collectionCollect current legislation, regulations, official forms, court guidance, approved explanations and verified procedural information.

    Purpose

    To build the legal knowledge repository from authoritative, current sources, with every item recorded using the metadata in section 4 and reviewed before it is used.

    Sources to collect for the protection order module

    Activities

    • Obtaining each source from its official origin, such as the Department of Justice, the Government Gazette or the Information Regulator, and recording where it came from.
    • Confirming that each source is the current version and recording its effective date.
    • Extracting the relevant sections and writing an approved plain-language explanation for each.
    • Entering every source as a record with the fields in section 4, starting with the status "draft".
    • Legal review and approval of each record, with the reviewer and review date recorded.
    • Writing evaluation cases with the legal team: realistic but fictional scenarios with the expected result, including difficult and unusual cases. Real applicants' cases are never used.

    Outputs

    • A legal knowledge repository with every source recorded, reviewed and approved.
    • Approved plain-language explanations and glossary.
    • A first set of evaluation cases.

    Who is involved

    The legal team (sourcing and approval), the AI Lead (structure and records), the Tech Lead (data entry and checks), native-speaker reviewers (translations) and the safeguarding lead (safety content).

    Complete when: every source needed for the first module is recorded with the status "approved", and none of the sources the module relies on is marked "draft" or "under review".

  3. Workflow and architecture designDesign the questionnaire, branching logic, data model, legal rules, document-generation process and integration points.

    Purpose

    To turn the approved process map and sources into a precise design that can be built and tested, and that the legal team can read and approve without reading code.

    Activities

    • Writing every question in plain language, with an explanation of why it is asked, in each supported language.
    • Defining for each question whether it is required or optional, what counts as a valid answer, and what happens if the applicant does not know or does not want to answer.
    • Defining the branches, for example when the applicant is applying for someone else, when children are involved, or when the matter is urgent.
    • Writing the legal rules, each linked to the source it comes from.
    • Mapping every answer to the exact box on the official form, including tick boxes.
    • Designing the data model for case information, with the minimum fields the form requires and nothing more.
    • Defining where AI is used and where it is not, following section 7, and how the applicant reviews any AI-assisted wording.
    • Defining the integration points: the website, WhatsApp, the AI services, document generation, and the safety and support services.
    • Completing the data protection assessment of the design with the Information Officer.

    Outputs

    • The workflow definition, written in a structured, readable format.
    • The legal rules, each linked to an approved source.
    • The field mapping to Form 6 and Form 6A.
    • The case data model and the data protection assessment.
    • The technical architecture, including hosting, security and integration points.

    Who is involved

    The AI Lead (design), the legal team (rules and questions), the Information Officer (data protection), the safeguarding lead (sensitive questions and safety pathways) and native-speaker reviewers (questions in each language).

    Complete when: the legal team has approved the workflow, rules and field mapping, and the Information Officer has signed off the data protection assessment.

  4. Build the first legal moduleImplement the protection order workflow, connect it to the legal knowledge service and build document generation.

    Purpose

    To build the protection order module on the shared platform, so that later legal modules can reuse the same services.

    Activities

    • Building the shared platform services: safety checks, languages, voice input and listening, channels, consent and usage counters.
    • Implementing the workflow engine to run the approved workflow definition.
    • Connecting the legal knowledge service so that only approved records are used, with source references.
    • Building document generation on the official fillable form, with completeness checks and a review step for the applicant.
    • Adding the "Things to bring to court" checklist and the "what happens next" information.
    • Implementing the privacy and security controls from section 8.
    • Deploying to GRIT's own hosting accounts, with keys and settings held securely outside the code.
    • Building automated tests that run after every change, so that changes to the workflow or AI model cannot silently break the module.

    Outputs

    • A working protection order module in a test environment.
    • Generated documents that match the official forms.
    • Automated tests and technical documentation.

    Who is involved

    The AI Lead and Tech Lead, with developer support as budgeted.

    Complete when: the full workflow can be completed end to end in the test environment in every supported language, and produces a correct, complete official form.

  5. Testing and legal validationTest standard cases, unusual circumstances, missing information, applicants acting on behalf of others, child-related scenarios and contradictory answers.

    Purpose

    To show, before any real applicant uses the module, that it is accurate, complete, safe and understandable.

    What is tested

    • Standard cases: typical applications completed correctly from start to finish.
    • Unusual circumstances: uncommon relationships, several respondents or incidents, applicants without identity documents.
    • Missing information: applicants who do not know dates, addresses or the respondent's details.
    • Applicants acting on behalf of others: the consent and capacity requirements are applied correctly.
    • Child-related scenarios: children affected, and applications involving maintenance or contact.
    • Contradictory answers: the system notices contradictions and asks the applicant rather than choosing for them.
    • Safety: danger, self-harm and distress are always met with the approved safety responses.
    • Language: conversations in each supported language, mixed languages and spoken input, with the form completed faithfully in English.
    • Faithfulness: the generated documents contain only what the applicant said, without added, softened or strengthened facts.
    • Privacy and security: data is handled as designed, and nothing is stored or shared that should not be.
    • Usability: testing with people who represent the intended users, including people with limited reading ability, supported by a counsellor.

    Outputs

    • A test report, with every issue found and how it was resolved.
    • A legal validation sign-off of the generated documents and explanations.
    • A safeguarding sign-off of the safety responses.

    Who is involved

    The legal team, the safeguarding lead, native-speaker reviewers, the AI Lead and Tech Lead, and user testers supported by a counsellor.

    Complete when: all agreed tests pass, no serious issue remains open, and the legal and safeguarding leads have signed off.

  6. Controlled deploymentPilot with appropriate users and legal support, review performance and errors, and expand only after the required approvals.

    Purpose

    To learn from real use in a safe, supported setting before the module is offered widely.

    Activities

    • Running a pilot with partner organisations, such as paralegals, shelters and court support services, so that pilot users have a trained person available to help them.
    • Monitoring content-free usage counters: how many applications are started and completed, which languages and channels are used, and where people stop.
    • Collecting feedback from pilot users and the people supporting them.
    • Reviewing errors and near-misses regularly with the legal and safeguarding leads, and correcting them.
    • Re-running the evaluation tests after every change before it reaches pilot users.
    • Agreeing the criteria for wider release, and using what the pilot shows to choose the next legal module.

    Outputs

    • A pilot report covering use, errors, feedback and changes made.
    • A decision on wider release, approved by GRIT's leadership.
    • A recommendation for the next legal module.

    Who is involved

    Partner organisations and their staff, the legal team, the safeguarding lead, the AI Lead and Tech Lead, and GRIT's leadership for the release decision.

    Complete when: the pilot has met the agreed criteria and GRIT's leadership has approved wider release.

10. Decisions and information required from GRIT

The following questions need to be resolved with the legal, product and technical teams before the architecture and data collection approach are finalised. The second column explains how each answer affects the design.

QuestionWhy it matters
1. Which exact legal process should the first version of inFORMed support?Determines the legislation, forms, workflow and evaluation cases to prepare first.
2. Is the first version exclusively for South Africa, or should it support other jurisdictions?Other jurisdictions would need their own legal sources, rules, forms and reviewers.
3. Who will provide and approve legal interpretations and workflow rules?Every legal record and rule needs a named reviewer before it can be used.
4. Which official forms and supporting documents must the tool prepare?Determines the document templates and the field mapping.
5. Which information is legally required, and which information is optional?Sets the minimum data the platform collects, and therefore its privacy footprint.
6. What exceptions and special circumstances must the workflow recognise?Each exception becomes a branch in the workflow and a set of test cases.
7. Will the first version only prepare documents, or is electronic submission a future requirement?Electronic submission would require integration with court systems and much stricter identity and security controls.
8. What should happen when a user cannot answer a question or does not have the required information?Decides whether questions can be skipped and how gaps are shown on the documents and explained to the applicant.
9. Will legal practitioners or trained support workers review applications before users download or submit them?If yes, case information must be shared with reviewers, which requires consent, secure storage, access control and audit logs.
10. What personal information must be retained, for how long, and who is authorised to access it?Determines whether case information is stored at all, and the retention, deletion and access rules if it is.

11. Initial design principles

Legal accuracy first

Use current, authoritative sources and require legal approval for legal rules, explanations and document templates.

Structured before generative

Use explicit workflows and validation for legally significant steps. Use AI to support, not replace, these controls.

User control

Users must be able to review, correct and approve information and documents prepared on their behalf.

Privacy by design

Separate case information from general legal knowledge and apply strict controls to sensitive information.

Modular and extensible

Build shared platform services and independent legal modules that can be added and maintained separately.

Traceable and testable

Connect legal content and rules to their sources, versions, approvals and evaluation tests.

13. Glossary

Protection order
A court order that prohibits a person from committing domestic violence and related acts. An interim order can be granted quickly; a final order is decided on the return date.
Applicant and respondent
The applicant is the person asking for protection. The respondent is the person the order is made against.
Interim order and return date
A temporary order granted before the respondent is heard, and the later court date on which the magistrate decides whether to make the order final.
Workflow engine
The part of the platform that decides which questions are asked, in what order, and which are required.
Legal rules engine
The part of the platform that applies approved legal and procedural rules, each linked to its source.
Legal knowledge service
The repository of approved, versioned legal sources and explanations used by the platform.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation: searching approved content for relevant passages and giving them to the AI, so that answers come from verified sources with references.
Evaluation data
Fictional test cases with expected results, used to measure accuracy, completeness and safety. Never real applicants' cases.
Special personal information
Information that POPIA protects more strictly, including information about health and alleged criminal behaviour.
Information Officer
The person responsible for an organisation's compliance with POPIA.
